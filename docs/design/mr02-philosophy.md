# Design philosophy: "Sân Chia Đôi" (The Split Pitch)

A visual movement for football result cards, written before any layout.

Football is territory divided and decided: two halves, two boxes, one line
in the middle that both sides must cross. "Sân Chia Đôi" expresses a result
the way a pitch expresses a match — as ground split between a winner and a
loser, with the scoreline as the monument standing on the border.

Space and form: the canvas is a pitch seen from above at night. One field,
two territories. The winner occupies the larger ground in broadcast coral;
the loser keeps the dark ink. A single thin paper rule marks the halfway
line, and a faint center circle — the most universal football glyph — sits
behind everything like a watermark. No boxes around content, no cards, no
chrome: territory itself is the layout.

Color and material: only the locked channel palette — ink night, coral
signal, paper chalk. Color is information, never decoration: coral means
"this side won," dimmed slate means "this side lost." Nothing else on the
canvas is allowed a strong color.

Scale and rhythm: one numeral dominates — the winner's goals, set so large
it becomes architecture. Everything else whispers: team names anchored to
their numbers, competition and venue in small quiet type, provenance as a
footnote. The eye lands on the score first, learns who won second, and
finds context last. That order is the whole rhythm.

Composition and balance: asymmetry with intent. The coral winner field takes
roughly three-fifths of the height; the ink loser field takes the rest. Type
is set left in the broadcast condensed voice, the way score bugs stack names
over numbers. Nothing is centered by default — centering is what generic
templates do when they have nothing to say.

Visual hierarchy through restraint: a result card answers three questions —
who won, by how much, in which match. Each gets exactly one visual element.
Anything that does not answer those questions is removed, including
decorative dividers, redundant labels, and filler graphics. The work must
look labored over for hours by someone at the top of broadcast design:
every alignment exact, every size deliberate, nothing overlapping, nothing
accidental.

Text as rare gesture: Vietnamese names and labels stay in the channel's
locked typefaces. Only the numerals — pure latin digits with no diacritics —
may wear a different display face, because the number is not text here. It
is the monument.
